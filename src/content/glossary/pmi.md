Suppose you buy a $300,000 home with a conventional loan. With $30,000 down (10%), your [LTV](/glossary/loan-to-value-ratio/) is 90% and PMI is likely to be required. With $60,000 down (20%), PMI is generally not required on a conventional loan. Premiums depend on the lender, loan and credit profile, so ask for the number on your Loan Estimate.

According to the CFPB, PMI is arranged by the lender, provided by private insurers and most commonly paid as a monthly premium added to your mortgage payment, though some loans use an up-front premium or both. It can help you qualify for a loan you might not otherwise get, but it raises the cost ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-private-mortgage-insurance-en-122/)). The premium is part of your housing payment for [DTI](/glossary/front-end-dti/).

PMI is often confused with homeowners insurance, which protects the home and you and is separate. PMI protects only the lender: if you fall behind, you can still lose the home. Government-backed loans such as FHA use their own mortgage insurance rather than PMI. Compare costs with the [mortgage calculator](/mortgage-calculator/), and see how [escrow](/glossary/escrow/) spreads taxes and insurance across your monthly payment.
