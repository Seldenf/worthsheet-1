A typical report lists personal information, your credit accounts (type, limit, balance, payment history and open and close dates), collection items, public records such as bankruptcies, and inquiries, meaning the companies that have accessed your report ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-report-en-309/)). Most people have more than one report, because creditors are not required to report to every credit reporting company.

Lenders use credit reports to decide whether to lend and at what interest rate. For a mortgage, the report is the main record behind a lender's view of your [character](/glossary/character-credit/). You can view your reports from Equifax, Experian and TransUnion for free at [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/), and checking your own report does not affect your score.

A report is not a score: the report holds the data, and a [credit score](/glossary/credit-score/) is a number calculated from it. The CFPB's list of what appears on a report does not include your income, so lenders collect that separately. Learn what mortgage lenders look at in [this guide](/what-do-mortgage-lenders-look-for-on-your-credit-report/).
