Say a lender issues a pre-approval letter for up to $350,000. That tells a seller you are likely to get financing in that range, but it is not a loan offer: the final decision comes after the application, the appraisal and [underwriting](/glossary/underwriting/). The CFPB notes that some lenders issue a letter valid for a set period, and that a letter rests on stated assumptions ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/whats-the-difference-between-a-prequalification-letter-and-a-preapproval-letter-en-127/)).

Lenders usually ask for documents (income, assets, debts) before issuing a pre-approval, and they may check your credit. Many people get one when they are ready to shop seriously, but getting pre-approved earlier can reveal credit problems while there is time to fix them.

Lenders use "pre-approval" and "[pre-qualification](/glossary/pre-qualification/)" inconsistently. The CFPB's advice is not to worry about which word a lender uses, so ask what the letter is based on, including whether your information was verified. A [personal financial statement](/glossary/personal-financial-statement/) helps you organize what you will be asked for. See the [readiness checklist](/mortgage-readiness-checklist/).
