The CFPB's example: a $1,500 mortgage payment, a $100 auto loan payment and $400 of other debt payments total $2,000 a month. Divided by $6,000 of gross monthly income, that is 33% ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-to-income-ratio-en-1791/)). Gross means before taxes and other deductions.

Lenders use DTI to judge whether you can handle a new payment. Limits differ by loan product and lender. Fannie Mae, for instance, sets a maximum of 36% for manually underwritten loans (up to 45% when credit score and reserve requirements are met) and 50% for loans run through Desktop Underwriter ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-02/debt-income-ratios)). The CFPB's 2020 General QM rule removed the old 43% DTI limit, so 43% is no longer a universal ceiling.

DTI is made of two ratios: [front-end DTI](/glossary/front-end-dti/) (housing only) and [back-end DTI](/glossary/back-end-dti/) (all debts). It also has nothing to do with credit utilization, and everyday spending such as groceries is not counted. Run your own numbers with the [DTI calculator](/debt-to-income-ratio-calculator/).
