Suppose you co-sign a $15,000 car loan for a family member. As long as they keep paying, you owe nothing, so the loan is not one of your regular [liabilities](/glossary/liabilities/). But if they stop, the lender can come to you for the balance. That possible obligation is a contingent liability.

Lenders ask about them because they can become real debts. SBA Form 413 has a dedicated section for contingent liabilities, covering items such as being an endorser or co-maker, legal claims and judgments, a provision for federal income tax and other special debt ([SBA Form 413](https://www.sba.gov/document/sba-form-413-personal-financial-statement)). They are typically reported separately from the balance-sheet liabilities. How a lender treats a co-signed debt in your [DTI](/glossary/debt-to-income-ratio/) depends on program rules, so disclose it and ask.

Leaving contingent liabilities off a statement is a common mistake, since they feel hypothetical. Because a [personal financial statement](/glossary/personal-financial-statement/) is a signed representation, completeness matters. Details for each line are in the [Form 413 walkthrough](/how-to-fill-out-sba-form-413/).
