Capacity asks whether your income can cover the new payment on top of what you already owe. Suppose gross income is $5,000 a month and existing debt payments are $1,000. A new $400 loan payment brings total debt payments to $1,400, or 28% of income. If existing payments were $2,000, the same loan would bring DTI to 48%, leaving much less room.

Lenders commonly turn capacity into a number with the [debt-to-income ratio](/glossary/debt-to-income-ratio/), shown as [front-end](/glossary/front-end-dti/) and [back-end](/glossary/back-end-dti/) DTI ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-to-income-ratio-en-1791/)). They also check that your income is stable and documented. Business lenders may look at the business's cash flow as well; ask what the lender uses.

Capacity is about income, not savings: a high earner with large debt payments can have weak capacity, and someone with savings but little income can too. The savings side is [capital](/glossary/capital-credit/). Overview: [five C's of credit](/glossary/five-cs-of-credit/).
