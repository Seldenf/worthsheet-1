If you buy a $300,000 home and pay $60,000 in cash, your down payment is 20% and you borrow $240,000. A smaller down payment means a bigger loan and a higher [loan-to-value ratio](/glossary/loan-to-value-ratio/).

The required amount depends on the loan and the lender. According to the CFPB, generally the larger the down payment, the lower the rate and the more likely you are to be approved, and if you cannot put 20% down lenders usually require [private mortgage insurance](/glossary/pmi/) or an FHA, VA or USDA loan ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-kind-of-down-payment-do-i-need-how-does-the-amount-of-down-payment-i-make-affect-the-terms-of-my-mortgage-loan-en-120/)). Some programs allow much less: FHA loans allow as little as 3.5% down for borrowers with a credit score of 580 or higher, and the VA reports that nearly 90% of VA-backed loans are made with no down payment ([VA](https://www.va.gov/housing-assistance/home-loans/)).

The down payment is not the only up-front cost. Closing costs, moving and early repairs also take cash, and lenders look at your remaining [liquid assets](/glossary/liquid-assets/) too. Twenty percent is a target to avoid PMI, not a rule for every loan. See the [readiness checklist](/mortgage-readiness-checklist/).
