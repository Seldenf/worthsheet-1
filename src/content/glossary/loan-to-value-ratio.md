Example: you agree to buy a home for $300,000 and borrow $240,000, but the appraisal comes in at $290,000. The lender uses the lower value, so LTV is $240,000 ÷ $290,000 ≈ 82.8%, not the 80% you would get from the price alone. A bigger [down payment](/glossary/down-payment/) lowers LTV.

According to the CFPB, lenders use LTV to decide whether to lend, how much you can borrow and what rate to offer, and a higher LTV usually means a higher rate and may mean [private mortgage insurance](/glossary/pmi/) or a government-backed loan ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-loan-to-value-ratio-and-how-does-it-relate-to-my-costs-en-121/)). It measures the lender's risk, since the home is the [collateral](/glossary/collateral/).

LTV is often confused with the down payment percentage or with equity. They are related (LTV = 100% minus your equity or down payment share, when price and value match), but only LTV uses the lower of price and appraisal. The WorthSheet [readiness assessment](/mortgage-readiness-assessment/) includes LTV as one of its five criteria.
