A PFS is dated because every number on it is only true on that day. Suppose that on June 30 you own $85,000 of assets (savings, a car and a retirement account) and owe $40,000 (a car loan and credit cards). Your PFS for June 30 shows a net worth of $45,000. Those figures are a made-up illustration, not a benchmark.

Who asks for one varies. Small-business lenders and the SBA collect a PFS from owners and guarantors, and banks use their own forms for some business and large personal loans. A standard U.S. mortgage application already asks for your assets and liabilities (see the [Uniform Residential Loan Application instructions](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/17391/display)), so whether a lender wants a separate PFS depends on the lender and the loan. Ask before you build one.

The most common mix-up is with its synonyms: a PFS, a [net worth statement](/glossary/net-worth-statement/) and a [personal balance sheet](/glossary/personal-balance-sheet/) are essentially the same report. [Net worth](/glossary/net-worth/) is only the final number, while the PFS lists every [asset](/glossary/assets/) and [liability](/glossary/liabilities/) behind it. For the SBA's version, see [SBA Form 413](https://www.sba.gov/document/sba-form-413-personal-financial-statement). You can fill in a ready-made one with the [free WorthSheet template](/download/).
