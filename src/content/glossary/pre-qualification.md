For example, you tell a lender your income is $90,000 and you have about $600 of monthly debt payments, and it responds with an estimate of what you could borrow. Nobody has checked pay stubs or bank statements, so the figure is a starting point for planning.

The CFPB says some lenders offer a prequalification letter based on unverified information you report and issue a preapproval letter only on verified information, but the terms are used differently by different lenders. Either way, the letter is not a guaranteed loan offer ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/whats-the-difference-between-a-prequalification-letter-and-a-preapproval-letter-en-127/)). Lenders may check your credit when issuing one, and a lender that evaluates your creditworthiness and says you do not qualify must give you an adverse action notice.

The common confusion is treating the word as a guarantee of a standard process. There is no universal difference in meaning, so ask what the lender verified. The stronger letter is usually a [pre-approval](/glossary/pre-approval/), and final approval only comes from [underwriting](/glossary/underwriting/).
