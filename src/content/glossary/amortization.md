Take a $240,000 mortgage at a hypothetical 6% fixed rate over 30 years. The payment of principal and interest is $1,438.92. In month one, interest is $240,000 × 0.5% = $1,200, so only $238.92 reduces the balance. Next month the balance is a little lower, so interest is a little lower and slightly more goes to principal. An amortization schedule lists that split for every payment.

The CFPB explains that early in a mortgage most of each payment pays interest, and over time more goes to principal; lenders use a standard formula so the loan is paid off exactly at the end of the term ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-does-paying-down-a-mortgage-work-en-1943/)). With a typical fixed-rate loan the principal-and-interest payment stays constant while the split changes.

Do not confuse amortization with the total monthly payment, which usually also includes taxes and insurance, or with the accounting use of the word for spreading the cost of intangible assets. The [mortgage calculator](/mortgage-calculator/) shows a yearly amortization table, and a longer term lowers the payment but increases total interest (see [conditions](/glossary/conditions-credit/)).
