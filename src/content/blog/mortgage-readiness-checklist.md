Mortgage readiness is being able to show a lender a credit history it can score, stable income that supports the payment, a manageable debt-to-income ratio, enough cash for the down payment, closing costs and reserves, and a clear picture of your net worth.

This checklist turns that sentence into five numbers you can measure today, each with a benchmark and a fix.

## What mortgage readiness means

Readiness is your own audit of what an underwriter will verify later. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/mortgages/ready-to-buy-a-home/) frames it as yes/no questions: steady income, good credit, few long-term debts, savings for a down payment, and room in the budget for the mortgage, insurance and taxes. We group those into a self-check across five areas:

1. **Credit:** an accurate report and a healthy score band.
2. **Income and employment:** documented, stable, enough to carry the payment.
3. **Down payment and loan-to-value:** how much of the price you fund yourself.
4. **Debt-to-income:** how much monthly income debts and the new payment would use.
5. **Net worth and reserves:** what you own, what you owe, and cash left after closing.

"Financial readiness" usually means the same thing in a broader sense: can your overall finances absorb the purchase? The checklist covers both.

## The 5-point mortgage readiness checklist

Bands labeled "WorthSheet" are our own educational bands; they mirror the Homebuyer Readiness tab of the free WorthSheet template. They are not lender approval rules, and having a PFS is not a lender criterion. Where a lender or agency publishes a real limit, we cite it.

### 1. Credit: pull reports, know your score band

Start with your reports, not your score. Errors can raise your rate, and the CFPB recommends reviewing reports early so there is time to fix mistakes. Request them from all three bureaus at [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/), the federally authorized source for free reports, which are available weekly. It gives you reports, not scores.

The WorthSheet bands follow common FICO ranges: Poor 300–579, Fair 580–669, Good 670–739, Very Good 740–799, Excellent 800–850.

Real minimums depend on the loan. The CFPB says that for some loans lenders want a minimum score of 620 unless you have a large down payment. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide sets 620 for manually underwritten fixed-rate loans (640 for adjustable-rate), while loans run through its Desktop Underwriter (DU) have no minimum score. Lenders may add stricter rules.

**Fix:** dispute errors, pay on time, keep card balances well below limits, and avoid new credit before you apply. See [what mortgage lenders look for on your credit report](/what-do-mortgage-lenders-look-for-on-your-credit-report/).

### 2. Income and employment: documentation and stability

Lenders care less about how high your income is than whether they can document it and expect it to continue. The CFPB asks whether you have at least two years of regular, steady income, and the Uniform Residential Loan Application (URLA) asks for at least two years of current and previous employment.

The template sorts household income into bands: under $40,000, $40,000–$75,000, $75,000–$150,000, $150,000–$300,000, and over $300,000. Treat them as a rough reference; the template notes income should be judged against local cost of living, and that stability and proof matter most.

**Fix:** gather two years of tax returns and W-2s, keep recent pay stubs, and tell the loan officer early about any expected change. Self-employed or irregular income usually needs extra documents.

### 3. Down payment and loan-to-value: know the program minimums

[Loan-to-value (LTV)](/glossary/loan-to-value-ratio/) is the loan divided by the home's value; a [down payment](/glossary/down-payment/) of 20% means 80% LTV. The CFPB's example for a $200,000 home: 20% down is $40,000, 5% is $10,000, and 3.5% is $7,000.

Treat 20% as a target, not a rule. The CFPB says 20% down improves your chances of approval, but you may qualify for a program needing much less, and a smaller down payment can mean paying mortgage insurance.

- **FHA:** a score of 580 or higher allows 3.5% down; with a score of 500–579, the maximum loan-to-value ratio is 90%, so you need at least 10% down. These tiers come from HUD Mortgagee Letter 2026-11; confirm current rules with an FHA-approved lender.
- **VA:** the VA says nearly 90% of VA-backed loans have no down payment, but you still need the required credit and income and a Certificate of Eligibility.

WorthSheet LTV bands: Poor above 92.5%, Fair above 85% to 92.5%, Good 75% to 85%, Very Good 60% to under 75%, Excellent under 60%. The [mortgage calculator](/mortgage-calculator/) shows how a bigger down payment changes the payment.

**Fix:** automate savings into a dedicated account. The CFPB packet checklist asks for statements showing two months of history for your down payment and a signed letter if part is a gift.

### 4. Debt-to-income: the 28/36 benchmark and Fannie Mae's limits

[Debt-to-income (DTI)](/glossary/debt-to-income-ratio/) compares monthly debt payments with gross monthly income. [Front-end DTI](/glossary/front-end-dti/) is the housing payment divided by income; [back-end DTI](/glossary/back-end-dti/) adds all other monthly debt payments.

The popular 28/36 rule says housing at or under 28% and total debt at or under 36%. It is a rule of thumb, not a lender rule. Fannie Mae's published maximum for manually underwritten loans is 36% of stable monthly income, up to 45% if the borrower meets the credit score and reserve requirements in its Eligibility Matrix; loans underwritten through DU can go to 50%. Government-backed loans follow their own agency rules.

Fannie Mae counts more than the mortgage: housing payment, installment debts lasting beyond ten months, revolving payments, lease payments regardless of how many months remain, and alimony or child support beyond ten months.

WorthSheet front-end bands: Poor above 28%, Good above 20% to 28%, Very Good 10% to 20%, Excellent under 10%. Back-end: Poor above 36%, Good above 30% to 36%, Very Good 20% to 30%, Excellent under 20%. Check yours with the [debt-to-income ratio calculator](/debt-to-income-ratio-calculator/).

**Fix:** pay off small installment loans and revolving balances first, since every monthly payment removed lowers back-end DTI. Avoid new debt mid-process: Fannie Mae requires re-underwriting when new debt or reduced income surfaces before closing and raises DTI beyond its tolerances.

### 5. Net worth and reserves: build a personal financial statement

Net worth is total assets minus total liabilities, and the CFPB lists savings and total assets among the factors lenders weigh. A [personal financial statement](/glossary/personal-financial-statement/) (PFS) puts every account and balance on one dated page, showing your [liquid assets](/glossary/liquid-assets/) and letting you check the numbers against statements. The standard application already asks for assets and liabilities, so a PFS is preparation, not a requirement; see [whether you need a PFS for a mortgage](/do-you-need-a-personal-financial-statement-for-a-mortgage/).

**Fix:** list every account in the free [WorthSheet template](/download/) and update it before each lender conversation.

## A worked example

These are the sample figures in the template's Homebuyer Readiness tab. They are illustrative, not typical.

- Income: $175,000 a year, or $14,583.33 a month.
- Price $800,000, down payment $115,000, loan $685,000, 30 years at 6.5%: principal and interest of $4,329.67 a month.
- Other monthly debt payments: $600. Credit score: 806.

| Check | Calculation | Result | WorthSheet band |
|---|---|---|---|
| Credit | 806 | 806 | Excellent |
| Income | $175,000 | $175,000 | Very Good |
| LTV | $685,000 ÷ $800,000 | 85.6% | Fair |
| Front-end DTI | $4,329.67 ÷ $14,583.33 | 29.7% | Poor |
| Back-end DTI | ($600 + $4,329.67) ÷ $14,583.33 | 33.8% | Good |

The template computes front-end DTI from principal and interest only. Taxes and insurance would raise it: with a hypothetical $1,000 a month added, front-end DTI would be 36.5% ($5,329.67 ÷ $14,583.33) and back-end 40.7% ($5,929.67 ÷ $14,583.33), above Fannie Mae's 36% manual limit but within its 50% DU maximum. Use real quotes for yours.

Now test a fix. A $160,000 down payment (20%) cuts the loan to $640,000 and the payment to about $4,045.24, so LTV falls to 80% (Good), front-end DTI to 27.7% (Good) and back-end DTI to 31.9% ($4,645.24 ÷ $14,583.33). The price: $45,000 more cash.

## Cash you need beyond the down payment

The CFPB's readiness list also asks whether you can cover closing costs, moving costs, furniture and repairs. Closing costs vary by lender, loan and location, so we give no percentage. Request a Loan Estimate instead: you need to submit only six items (name, income, Social Security number, property address, estimated home value and loan amount), and the lender must send one within three business days.

Then do the arithmetic. Cash to close is the down payment plus closing costs; reserves are liquid assets minus cash to close. With hypothetical liquid assets of $150,000, a $115,000 down payment and $20,000 of closing costs, you keep $15,000, about 3.5 months of the $4,329.67 payment ($15,000 ÷ $4,329.67 ≈ 3.46). Required reserves vary, so ask your lender.

## Documents to gather

The CFPB's loan application packet checklist includes:

- Pay stub for the last 30 days, W-2s and signed federal tax returns for two years
- Documentation of other income
- The two most recent bank statements
- Down payment source documents (two months of history; signed gift letter if applicable)
- Photo ID and Social Security number; proof of any name change
- For veterans, a VA Certificate of Eligibility

List every debt too: the URLA asks for all personal debt you owe or will owe before closing, including debts missing from your credit report. Ask your lender for its own checklist, since requirements vary.

## Signs you're not ready yet

- Card balances sit near their limits.
- Back-end DTI is above the limit for your loan.
- Savings would be near zero after closing.
- Credit report errors remain undisputed.
- You expect a job or income change soon.
- You cannot comfortably pay the full housing cost from today's budget.

Each sign maps to one of the five checks, and each has a fix.

## A simple 6-month plan

1. **Month 1:** pull all three reports, dispute errors, and list every account and debt in your PFS.
2. **Month 2:** track spending (the CFPB offers a free tracker) and set a target payment.
3. **Months 3–4:** pay down revolving balances and small loans; keep saving.
4. **Month 5:** assemble the document packet and request Loan Estimates.
5. **Month 6:** ask for pre-approval. The CFPB notes pre-approval letters are not guaranteed loan offers, and that applying earlier can reveal credit issues in time to fix them.
