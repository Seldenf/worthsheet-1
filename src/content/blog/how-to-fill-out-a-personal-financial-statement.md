To fill out a personal financial statement, list everything you own at its current market value, list everything you owe with current balances, subtract your liabilities from your assets to get your net worth, and sign and date the statement as of a single date.

Six steps get you there: pick the date, value your assets, list your liabilities, add contingent liabilities and unpaid taxes, check the math, then review and sign. This guide covers each one, with the valuation rules lenders expect and the mistakes that most often get a statement sent back. If you first need the background, see [what a personal financial statement is](/what-is-a-personal-financial-statement/); to see a completed one, open the [personal financial statement example](/personal-financial-statement-example/).

## What you need before you start

Collect these before you enter a single number, and your statement will be easier to defend:

- **Account statements** for every bank, brokerage and retirement account, from the date you are using or the closest one.
- **Loan and card statements** for your mortgage or HELOC, auto, student and other loans, and credit cards, showing current balances.
- **Property value support** for real estate and vehicles: a recent appraisal, comparable sales, an online estimate or a vehicle pricing guide.
- **Business ownership documents** if you own part of a private company: ownership percentage, a recent valuation or financial statements.
- **Life insurance statements** that show cash surrender value.
- **Documents for any guarantee or co-signed debt,** and for any pending claim or tax bill.

Lenders check what you tell them. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, for example, requires the lender to confirm that information provided by the borrower during the application is accurate and complete, and to document the assets used for qualifying. A statement that matches your account statements is far less likely to raise questions.

## Step 1: Choose your "as of" date

A personal financial statement is a snapshot, so choose one date and make every number match it. Using the end of the most recent month is simple, because your statements already line up with it. Write the date at the top of the statement.

How current it must be depends on the reader. SBA Form 413 says the information must be current within 120 days of submission for most programs, but SBA's SOP 50 10 8.1 asks lenders for guarantor statements dated within 90 days of loan approval, and a lender may set its own window. When in doubt, use fresh numbers dated shortly before you submit.

## Step 2: List assets at current value

For each asset, enter what it is worth today, not what you paid. The SBA's Form 413 is a useful model for how banks expect values to be shown, so the rules below follow it.

### Cash and savings

Enter checking, savings, money-market and CD balances from your statements. Form 413 asks you to omit cents, so rounding to whole dollars is normal.

### Retirement and investment accounts

Use the balance on your statement date. For stocks and bonds, Form 413 asks for market value and the date of the quotation, so note the date if you use a quote rather than a statement. Do not use the original purchase price.

### Real estate

Use present market value, not cost. Form 413 asks for present market value and, separately, original cost and the date purchased, so keep those two numbers apart. List each property on its own line, and enter its mortgage separately in the liabilities.

### Vehicles

Enter the present value, based on year, make, model and condition. Form 413 asks for year, make and model beside each automobile. Use trade-in or private-party value, not what you paid.

### Business interests and other property

Enter your share of a private business at a conservative value you can support, such as a recent valuation or the book value from its financial statements. List collectibles, jewelry and similar items at realistic resale value. If you cannot support a figure, use a lower number you can.

### Life insurance

Use the **cash surrender value only,** as Form 413 specifies. The death benefit is not an asset you own today.

## Step 3: List liabilities

For each debt, enter the **balance you owe,** not the monthly payment or the original loan amount. Pull the figure from the latest statement and list each creditor separately:

- credit cards;
- home loans (mortgage, HELOC);
- student loans;
- vehicle loans (auto, boat, RV);
- notes payable, such as personal loans and loans on other property;
- other obligations, such as alimony or child support.

Include small debts, including store cards and payment plans. If you share a debt with a co-signer or joint borrower, say so and ask the lender how it wants the shared amount shown. See the glossary for [liabilities](/glossary/liabilities/) and [assets](/glossary/assets/).

## Step 4: Add contingent liabilities and unpaid taxes

Two items are easy to overlook because they are not ordinary loans.

- **Unpaid taxes.** Income, property or other taxes you owe go on the liability side. Form 413 has a separate section for them.
- **Contingent liabilities.** These are obligations that may become yours: a loan you co-signed or personally guaranteed, a pending lawsuit or judgment, or income tax you expect to owe. Form 413 lists these as "As Endorser or Co-Maker," "Legal Claims & Judgments," "Provision for Federal Income Tax" and "Other Special Debt," and they are normally kept apart from total liabilities.

Many simple statements, including the WorthSheet template, do not have a separate contingent-liability block. If your lender wants them, attach a signed page that lists each one. For the formal list, see our [line-by-line guide to SBA Form 413](/how-to-fill-out-sba-form-413/) and the glossary entry for [contingent liabilities](/glossary/contingent-liabilities/).

## Step 5: Calculate net worth and check your math

Add up each category, then:

> **Net worth = total assets − total liabilities**

Then check your work:

1. **Category totals match the detail.** If you have two savings accounts, $12,000 at one bank and $4,500 at another, the savings total must be $12,000 + $4,500 = $16,500.
2. **Net worth plus liabilities equals assets.** If assets are $412,500 and liabilities are $186,200, net worth is $226,300, and $226,300 + $186,200 = $412,500. (Hypothetical numbers.)
3. **Numbers match your documents.** Compare each balance to the statement it came from, and confirm that the mortgage balance ties to the property it secures.

Form 413 builds this check in: it requires net worth to equal the total in the assets column once liabilities are added. If you want to see the arithmetic, read [how to calculate net worth](/how-to-calculate-net-worth/) or run the [net worth calculator](/net-worth-calculator/).

## Step 6: Review, sign and date

Read the statement once more as the lender would. Do totals add up? Is everything dated the same? Is anything missing that a bank statement or credit report will show? Then sign and date it. Attach supporting statements if the lender asks for them.

Sign only what you believe is accurate. Many lender and government forms carry a certification that the information is true and complete. SBA Form 413, for instance, prints penalties for knowingly false statements, including up to 30 years' imprisonment and a $1,000,000 fine under 18 U.S.C. § 1014 for statements to a federally insured institution. Careful, supportable estimates are fine; what matters is that you are honest about the values and that nothing material is left out.

## Common mistakes

- **Cost instead of market value,** especially for real estate, vehicles and securities.
- **Forgetting small debts,** such as store cards, medical payment plans and personal loans from relatives.
- **Mixing business and personal.** Keep personal assets and debts on a personal statement. List your business only as an ownership interest, and show any business debt you personally guaranteed as a guarantee.
- **A stale date.** Update the statement shortly before you apply; lenders may require fresh numbers.
- **Numbers that conflict with the credit report or bank statements.** If your statement omits a loan that appears on your credit report, expect questions.
- **Cents and rounding.** Omit cents consistently, as Form 413 does.

## Which format to use

- **The lender's own form.** If the lender sends one, use it. It tells you exactly what the lender needs.
- **A spreadsheet template.** Many lenders accept one if it lists the same information. Ask first.
- **SBA Form 413.** Required for 8(a) and WOSB, and the standard for SBA loans. For 7(a), 504 and surety purposes SBA accepts equivalent formats with the same data, so ask your lender which it prefers. See the [SBA Form 413 page](/sba-form-413/).
