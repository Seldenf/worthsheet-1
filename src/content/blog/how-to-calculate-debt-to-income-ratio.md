Your debt-to-income (DTI) ratio is your total monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income, expressed as a percentage — for example, $2,000 in monthly debt payments on $6,000 of gross monthly income is a 33% DTI.

That's the whole calculation. The harder parts are knowing which payments count, which income to use and what number a lender wants to see. This guide covers each of those, with worked examples you can copy.

## The DTI formula

> **DTI = monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income × 100**

Both numbers are monthly. The [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-to-income-ratio-en-1791/) describes DTI as all your monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income, which is generally what you earn before taxes and other deductions come out. It adds that different loan products and lenders have different DTI limits.

If your income is an annual salary, divide it by 12 first. A $90,000 salary is $7,500 per month.

## Step-by-step example

Start with the CFPB's own example.

1. **List your monthly debt payments.** A $1,500 mortgage payment, $100 for an auto loan and $400 for the rest of your debts add up to $1,500 + $100 + $400 = $2,000.
2. **Find your gross monthly income.** In the CFPB example it's $6,000.
3. **Divide.** $2,000 ÷ $6,000 = 0.333.
4. **Convert to a percentage.** Multiply by 100 to get a 33% DTI.

### A second example with a proposed new mortgage

Now suppose you're planning to buy a home. These numbers are hypothetical.

- Gross income: $90,000 a year, or $7,500 a month
- Proposed housing payment: $1,650 principal and interest + $300 property taxes + $150 homeowners insurance = $2,100
- Car loan: $420
- Student loan: $260
- Credit card minimum payments: $90

| Item | Monthly amount |
|---|---|
| Proposed housing payment (principal, interest, taxes, insurance) | $2,100 |
| Car loan | $420 |
| Student loan | $260 |
| Credit card minimums | $90 |
| **Total monthly debt** | **$2,870** |
| Gross monthly income | $7,500 |

The back-end (total) DTI is $2,870 ÷ $7,500 = 38.3%. The front-end (housing) DTI is $2,100 ÷ $7,500 = 28.0%. Both figures come up again below.

## Front-end vs. back-end DTI

Lenders may look at two versions of the ratio:

- **Front-end DTI (housing ratio):** your proposed monthly housing payment divided by gross monthly income. Housing here means principal, interest, property taxes and homeowners insurance (PITI), plus any association dues.
- **Back-end DTI (total ratio):** all monthly debt payments, including the housing payment, divided by gross monthly income.

The **28/36 rule** is a common rule of thumb: keep the front-end ratio at or below 28% and the back-end ratio at or below 36%. It's a guideline, not a requirement. Lenders and loan programs set their own limits, and some approve borrowers above those numbers. In the example above, the front-end ratio is exactly 28.0% and the back-end ratio of 38.3% is above 36%, so the household would be right at one benchmark and over the other.

For plain definitions of each ratio, see the glossary entries for [debt-to-income ratio](/glossary/debt-to-income-ratio/), [front-end DTI](/glossary/front-end-dti/) and [back-end DTI](/glossary/back-end-dti/).

**A note on the WorthSheet template.** The Homebuyer Readiness tab in the WorthSheet template computes its front-end figure from the mortgage's principal and interest payment only, using a payment formula. Its text describes the housing ratio as including taxes and insurance. Because of that, the tab's front-end result will read lower than a PITI-based ratio. If you want the full housing ratio, add your estimated taxes and insurance to the housing payment and divide by gross monthly income, as in the example above.

## What counts as debt in DTI

Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, [B3-6-02](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-02/debt-income-ratios), spells out what goes into a borrower's total monthly obligation for loans it purchases. It includes:

- **The housing payment** for your principal residence. If the home you're buying will be your primary residence, that means PITIA: principal, interest, taxes, insurance and association dues.
- **Installment debts** such as auto and student loans, and other mortgage debts, that extend beyond ten months. Payments that run ten months or less count too if they significantly affect your ability to meet your credit obligations.
- **Revolving debts** such as credit cards, using the monthly payment.
- **Lease payments**, regardless of the expiration date.
- **Alimony, child support or maintenance** that extends beyond ten months. (Fannie Mae allows alimony, but not child support or maintenance, to be deducted from income instead.)
- **Other recurring monthly obligations.**
- **Any net loss from a rental property.**

Everyday expenses such as groceries, utilities and phone bills aren't on that list.

Lenders can be more conservative than Fannie Mae. The guide says that's acceptable if they apply the same approach consistently, and gives the example of a lender calculating a higher minimum payment for a credit card than Fannie Mae requires. So the number your lender calculates may be a little higher than your own.

One more point: if you take on new debt during the loan process, the lender may recalculate. Fannie Mae requires lenders to re-underwrite a loan when newly discovered debt or reduced income pushes DTI beyond allowed tolerances, which is a good reason to hold off on financing a car or furniture until after closing.

## What income to use

Use gross income, meaning before taxes and deductions, as the CFPB explains. For a mortgage, Fannie Mae counts the total monthly income of all borrowers, to the extent that income is used to qualify for the loan, and it refers to the borrower's "stable monthly income."

In practice:

- **Salary:** divide annual pay by 12.
- **Hourly pay:** multiply the hourly rate by hours per week and 52 weeks, then divide by 12.
- **Variable pay, self-employment or rental income:** lenders document and average these differently, so ask your lender how it will treat yours.
- **Co-borrowers:** if two people apply, the lender combines their incomes and their debts.

Lenders verify income with pay stubs, tax returns and other documents. For more on that, see the overview of [what lenders review when reviewing a mortgage application](/what-do-banks-look-for-when-reviewing-a-mortgage-application/), where DTI is one of five main factors.

## What is a good DTI for a mortgage?

Lower is better, but there's no single magic number. For conventional loans backed by Fannie Mae:

| Underwriting method | Maximum total DTI |
|---|---|
| Manually underwritten | 36% of stable monthly income |
| Manually underwritten, with credit score and reserves from Fannie Mae's Eligibility Matrix | Up to 45% |
| Desktop Underwriter (DU) loan casefiles | 50% |

Source: [Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-6-02](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-02/debt-income-ratios). The same page notes exceptions for some transactions, and says government loans must follow their own agency's requirements. FHA, VA and USDA publish their own DTI guidelines, so ask a lender who offers those programs or read the program's published guidance rather than relying on a generic number.

Using the second example, the 38.3% back-end ratio would be above the 36% rule of thumb. For a manually underwritten Fannie Mae loan, it could still be possible if the credit score and reserve requirements in the Eligibility Matrix are met, and a DU recommendation allows up to 50%. A lender still looks at everything else in your file.

### What happened to the 43% limit?

You may still see 43% quoted as a DTI cap. It used to be part of the federal "General QM" definition under the Truth in Lending Act's Regulation Z, which defines a "qualified mortgage." In December 2020 the CFPB's [General QM final rule](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/final-rules/qualified-mortgage-definition-under-truth-lending-act-regulation-z-general-qm-loan-definition/) removed the 43% DTI limit from that definition and replaced it with price-based thresholds. So 43% is no longer a universal ceiling, although individual lenders and programs still set their own DTI limits.

## How to lower your DTI

Because DTI is a fraction, you can lower it by shrinking the top (debt payments) or growing the bottom (documented income).

- **Pay off or pay down debts with monthly payments.** In the second example, paying off the $420 car loan drops total monthly debt to $2,450. The back-end ratio falls from 38.3% to $2,450 ÷ $7,500 = 32.7%. Paying down revolving balances reduces minimum payments too.
- **Avoid new debt.** Every new payment adds to the numerator.
- **Increase documented income.** A raise, a second documented income or a co-borrower can help. If income in the example were $8,000 a month, the back-end ratio would be $2,870 ÷ $8,000 = 35.9%.
- **Adjust the loan size or term.** A smaller loan or a longer term lowers the monthly payment.

On that last point, a longer term has a cost. On a $200,000 loan at a hypothetical 6% rate, the principal-and-interest payment is about $1,199.10 over 30 years versus $1,687.71 over 15 years. That's $488.61 less per month, but total interest is about $231,676 for the 30-year loan against about $103,788 for the 15-year.
