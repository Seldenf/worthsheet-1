To calculate your net worth, add up everything you own (your assets) and subtract everything you owe (your liabilities): net worth = total assets − total liabilities.

That is the whole method. The work is in the details: deciding what to count, putting an honest value on each item, and doing it all as of one date. This guide walks through four steps, a worked example with the arithmetic shown, and the latest Federal Reserve numbers so you have something to compare against. If you would rather skip the arithmetic, the [net worth calculator](/net-worth-calculator/) or the [free WorthSheet template](/download/) does the adding for you.

## The net worth formula

> **Net worth = total assets − total liabilities**

A positive result means you own more than you owe. A negative result means your debts are larger than the value of what you own. Neither is a verdict on your finances: it is a snapshot. Someone with student loans and a first car often starts out negative, and the number usually moves as balances are paid down and savings grow.

If you want the plain definition rather than the calculation, see the glossary entry for [net worth](/glossary/net-worth/). The rest of this page is about doing the math correctly.

## Step 1: List your assets

An [asset](/glossary/assets/) is anything of value that you own. The WorthSheet template groups them into eight categories, which makes a good checklist:

- **Checking and savings accounts**: every bank account, including accounts at online banks.
- **Retirement accounts**: 401(k), 403(b), traditional, Roth and SEP IRAs, and pensions you can put a balance on.
- **Investment accounts**: brokerage and mutual-fund accounts, CDs, money-market accounts, government bonds, crypto and restricted company stock.
- **Real estate**: your home, a second home, rental or investment property.
- **Auto, boat and RV**: any vehicle you own.
- **Private business interest**: your ownership stake in a company that is not publicly traded.
- **Other**: an HSA, a 529 plan, annuities, the cash value of whole-life insurance, art and collectibles.

Write down the institution or address next to each line, so every number can be traced back to a statement. In total the template covers 27 asset types across those categories, so use its list as a prompt for things you might forget.

## Step 2: Put a realistic value on each asset

The common mistake is to enter what you paid rather than what the item is worth today. Official forms ask for current value, and you should too. SBA Form 413, the federal personal financial statement for small-business loans, is a useful model: it asks for real estate at present market value, stocks and bonds at market value on a quotation date, and life insurance at cash surrender value only.

### Real estate

Use the market value today, not the purchase price. A recent appraisal is best; otherwise use comparable recent sales or an online estimate and round conservatively. Do not net the mortgage out of this line. The home goes in at full value here, and the loan is listed separately in Step 3.

### Vehicles

Use what the vehicle would realistically sell or trade in for, based on year, make, model and mileage, not the sticker price. Vehicles lose value quickly, so an old figure can overstate your net worth by thousands.

### Retirement and investment accounts

Use the balance on the date you are measuring. Markets move daily, so pick your date first (Step 4) and read every balance as of that date or the closest statement. List cash-value life insurance at its cash surrender value, not the death benefit; Form 413 asks for cash surrender value only.

### Business interests and collectibles

These are the hardest to price and the easiest to inflate. Use a conservative figure you could document, such as a recent valuation, a buy-sell agreement, or an appraisal of the collectibles. If you cannot support a number, a lower, defensible value serves you better than an optimistic one, especially if a lender will read it.

## Step 3: List your liabilities

A [liability](/glossary/liabilities/) is any amount you owe. Enter the current balance, not the monthly payment. The template's 13 liability categories make a good checklist:

- Credit cards
- Home loans: mortgage and HELOC
- Student loans: subsidized and non-subsidized
- Auto, boat and RV loans
- Notes payable: personal loans, business loans you have personally guaranteed, and loans on other property
- Other obligations: for example, personal bankruptcy obligations and alimony or child support

Pull the balance from the most recent statement. Small debts count too: a store card or a medical payment plan belongs on the list. If a loan is shared with a co-signer or spouse, list your share and note the arrangement.

## Step 4: Subtract and record the date

Add up the asset values, add up the liability balances, and subtract. Then write the date next to the result.

The date matters because net worth is a snapshot. Your savings balance, your stock holdings and your credit-card balance all change from week to week, so a net worth figure with no date can't be compared with anything. SBA Form 413 builds this in: it computes net worth as total assets minus total liabilities and requires the date the information is current as of.

## Worked example

The numbers below are a hypothetical example, not real data. They use the same categories as the template.

| Assets | Amount | Liabilities | Amount |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Checking accounts | $18,400 | Mortgage | $168,000 |
| Savings accounts | $45,000 | Student loans | $11,200 |
| Retirement accounts (401(k) and IRA) | $149,100 | Credit cards | $7,000 |
| Real estate (primary home, market value) | $200,000 | | |
| **Total assets** | **$412,500** | **Total liabilities** | **$186,200** |

The arithmetic:

- Assets: $18,400 + $45,000 + $149,100 + $200,000 = **$412,500**
- Liabilities: $168,000 + $11,200 + $7,000 = **$186,200**
- Net worth: $412,500 − $186,200 = **$226,300**

Notice two things. First, the house is $200,000 of the assets and its mortgage is $168,000 of the liabilities, so the equity in the home is $200,000 − $168,000 = $32,000. Most of this example's net worth sits in retirement accounts ($149,100) rather than in the home. Second, the cash a lender could count as readily available is far smaller than net worth: checking plus savings is $18,400 + $45,000 = $63,400.

### Negative net worth is common early in life

Here is a second hypothetical example, for someone just out of school:

| Assets | Amount | Liabilities | Amount |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Checking account | $2,300 | Student loans | $24,800 |
| Savings account | $1,200 | Credit card | $1,900 |
| Car (private-party value) | $6,500 | | |
| **Total assets** | **$10,000** | **Total liabilities** | **$26,700** |

Net worth: $10,000 − $26,700 = **−$16,700**. That is a normal starting point, and it is exactly why the figure is worth tracking: each loan payment and each deposit moves it in the right direction.

## What is a typical net worth?

The most authoritative benchmark is the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), conducted every three years. The 2025 survey was released on October 9, 2026. Its headline numbers, in 2025 dollars:

- Median family net worth: **$215,900**, up 2 percent from the 2022 survey.
- Mean (average) family net worth: **$1.24 million**, up 7 percent. The mean is far above the median because a small number of very wealthy families pull it up, which is why the median is the better "typical" figure.
- For families that own a home, median net housing value (home value minus home-secured debt) was **$230,000**.

The Fed's report also breaks median net worth down by the age of the family's reference person (table 2 of its report, thousands of 2025 dollars):

| Age | Median net worth, 2025 | Change from 2022 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Under 35 | $33,000 | −23% |
| 35–44 | $155,600 | +5% |
| 45–54 | $253,700 | −6% |
| 55–64 | $411,900 | +3% |
| 65–74 | $431,300 | −4% |
| 75 and older | $504,900 | +37% |

Housing status matters as much as age: the same table puts median net worth at $458,900 for homeowners and $10,600 for the "renter or other" group.

Treat these as benchmarks, not goals. The SCF measures families, not individuals, and the Fed itself notes that net worth alone does not fully capture a family's economic situation. A person with a high income, a young family and a new mortgage can sit below the median for their age and still be on a strong path. The useful comparison is mostly with your own past numbers.

## Net worth versus liquid assets

Net worth tells you how much you own after debts, but it does not tell you how much of it you could use in a hurry. Your home and your retirement accounts count fully toward net worth, yet neither is easy to spend. [Liquid assets](/glossary/liquid-assets/) are the part you can turn into cash quickly: checking, savings, money-market funds and similar holdings.

Lenders look at both. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau lists savings and total assets among the factors mortgage lenders weigh alongside income and debt. That is why a loan application often asks for your savings balances separately from your overall picture, and why a one-page [personal financial statement](/what-is-a-personal-financial-statement/) lists accounts individually instead of showing only the total.

## How often to recalculate

- **Once or twice a year** is plenty if you just want to track progress. Pick a consistent date, such as January 1, so year-over-year comparisons are fair.
- **Quarterly** works well if you are paying down debt or saving for a goal.
- **Before any loan application.** Lenders ask for current figures, and a statement prepared months ago may not be accepted. SBA Form 413, for instance, says the information must be current within 120 days of submission for most programs.
