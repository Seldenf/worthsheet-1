When reviewing a loan application, lenders consider the five C's of credit: your credit history (character), your income and existing debts (capacity), your savings and net worth (capital), the asset that secures the loan (collateral), and the loan's purpose and terms (conditions).

The five C's are an industry convention, not a law or a regulation. Each lender applies its own policies and its own mix of the five, but the same questions come up again and again, whether you're applying for a credit card, a personal loan, a car loan or a business loan. The [FDIC's Money Smart for Small Business training](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-resource-center/mssb-m5-pg.pdf) teaches the same five factors as the framework lenders use when underwriting a loan.

This guide shows where each C appears in your paperwork, what lenders actually check, and what you can fix before you apply.

## The short answer: the five C's of credit

| The C | Question the lender asks | Where it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| **Character** | Do you have a record of repaying what you borrow? | Credit report and credit score |
| **Capacity** | Can you afford the new payment on top of your current debts? | Pay stubs, tax returns, debt list (debt-to-income ratio) |
| **Capital** | Do you have savings and net worth to fall back on? | Bank and investment statements, personal financial statement |
| **Collateral** | What can the lender claim if the loan isn't repaid? | Appraisal, title, vehicle or equipment valuation |
| **Conditions** | What is the loan for, and do the amount, rate and term make sense? | The loan request itself, plus broader factors like the economy |

Read the table left to right and you have the whole review in miniature: the lender looks at your history, your budget, your cushion, the security behind the loan and the loan itself.

## Which items do credit card and lending companies use to decide whether to lend?

This is the question many students and borrowers type into a search box, so here is the direct answer. Credit card issuers and lending companies typically use:

- **Your credit report and credit score.** They show how you have handled credit in the past.
- **Your income and employment.** Lenders want to see current, reliable income that can support the payment.
- **Your existing debts.** Compared with your income, they produce your [debt-to-income (DTI) ratio](/how-to-calculate-debt-to-income-ratio/).
- **Your savings and assets.** Cash and other assets show a cushion if income dips.
- **Collateral.** For secured loans, the asset the lender can claim if you stop paying.
- **The loan amount, purpose and terms.** A realistic request for a clear purpose is easier to approve than a vague or oversized one.

Those six items are the five C's with the details spelled out: the first is character, the second and third are capacity, the fourth is capital, the fifth is collateral and the sixth is conditions.

## The five C's in detail

### Character (credit history)

Character is your track record. Lenders read it from your credit reports, which are maintained by Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, and from the score calculated from them.

According to [myFICO](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/whats-in-your-credit-score), FICO Scores are built from five categories of credit-report data:

- Payment history: 35%
- Amounts owed: 30%
- Length of credit history: 15%
- New credit: 10%
- Credit mix: 10%

Notice what is missing. myFICO says your salary, occupation, employer and employment history are not factors in a FICO Score, although lenders may weigh them separately. Character is about how you handled credit, not how much you earn. For a closer look at what is on a report and what lenders flag, see [what mortgage lenders look for on your credit report](/what-do-mortgage-lenders-look-for-on-your-credit-report/). The report items apply to other loans too. For the term itself, see the glossary entry for [character](/glossary/character-credit/).

### Capacity (income versus debts)

Capacity is your ability to repay from income. Lenders compare your monthly debt payments, including the new loan, with your gross monthly income. That comparison is your DTI ratio.

Here is a simple, hypothetical illustration (not real data). Suppose a borrower earns $5,000 a month before taxes and requests a $12,000, 36-month personal loan at 10% APR. The payment on that loan works out to about $387 a month.

| | Monthly payment |
|---|---|
| Car loan | $350 |
| Student loan | $200 |
| Credit card minimum | $75 |
| **Existing debts** | **$625** |
| New personal loan (estimate) | $387 |
| **Debts including new loan** | **$1,012** |

Before the new loan, the ratio is $625 ÷ $5,000 = 12.5%. After it, the ratio is about $1,012 ÷ $5,000 = 20.2%. Many lenders also include rent or a mortgage payment in this calculation, which would push the ratio higher, so the lender's own formula matters.

Try your own numbers with the [debt-to-income ratio calculator](/debt-to-income-ratio-calculator/). More on the term is in the glossary entry for [capacity](/glossary/capacity-credit/).

### Capital (savings, down payment, net worth)

Capital is what you have behind you: savings, investments, equity and the money you put into a purchase. A borrower with several months of expenses in the bank is a lower risk than one living paycheck to paycheck on the same income, because a surprise bill is less likely to cause a missed payment.

Capital is measured by your assets minus your liabilities, which is your net worth. You can estimate yours with the [net worth calculator](/net-worth-calculator/). Glossary: [capital](/glossary/capital-credit/).

### Collateral

Collateral is an asset that secures the loan, such as a car for an auto loan or a home for a home loan. If you stop paying, the lender can claim it. That reduces the lender's loss and is the reason secured loans usually cost less than unsecured ones.

Lenders verify collateral independently. They may order an appraisal or title search, or look up a vehicle's value, because the asset needs to be worth enough to cover the loan. See the glossary entry for [collateral](/glossary/collateral/).

### Conditions

Conditions cover the loan itself and its surroundings: how much you want to borrow, what you will use it for, the interest rate and term, and sometimes broader factors such as economic conditions or the state of your industry for a business loan.

A clear, specific purpose and a loan size that fits your income make a stronger application. Glossary: [conditions](/glossary/conditions-credit/). For the full framework in one place, see [the five C's of credit](/glossary/five-cs-of-credit/).

## How the C's change by loan type

Because the five C's are a convention and not a rulebook, the emphasis shifts with the product. The patterns below are general; every lender sets its own policies.

### Credit cards

Credit cards are usually unsecured, so there is no collateral to evaluate. Issuers lean mostly on character (your credit report and score) and capacity (your income relative to your obligations).

### Personal loans

Unsecured personal loans look much like cards, but the lender also weighs the amount and term you request. Expect character, capacity and conditions to carry most of the weight, with capital as a tie-breaker.

### Secured loans (auto, home)

When a loan is secured by a car or a home, collateral moves to the front. The lender will care how much the asset is worth compared with the loan, and how much of your own money you are putting in.

Fannie Mae's underwriting guide, which governs many home loans, doesn't use the five-C label, but its [comprehensive risk assessment](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-1-01/comprehensive-risk-assessment) looks at the same ground: equity investment, credit history, liquid reserves and reliable, recurring income.

### Business and SBA loans

Business lenders add the company's cash flow to the picture, and they often look at the owners' personal finances too. The U.S. Small Business Administration says a business must be [creditworthy and demonstrate a reasonable ability to repay](https://www.sba.gov/loans/7a-loans/) to be eligible for a 7(a) loan, and that you apply through a participating lender, not through SBA.

## Where a personal financial statement fits

A personal financial statement (PFS) lists what you own, what you owe, and the difference between the two on a single date. That makes it a one-page view of two C's at once: your liquid assets and net worth (capital), and your liabilities (capacity).

Some lenders ask for a PFS, particularly for business loans and larger or more complex requests. Having one ready saves time, and building it forces you to find the numbers a lender will ask about anyway. ## How to strengthen each C before you apply

Pick the weakest C first. It's the one most likely to cost you an approval or a lower rate.

- **Character:** Get your free reports from AnnualCreditReport.com, dispute anything that isn't yours, and keep every payment on time. Per the CFPB, you can view your reports from all three bureaus [for free once a week](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-get-a-free-copy-of-my-credit-reports-en-5/).
- **Capacity:** Pay down revolving balances and avoid adding new debt, which lowers your DTI. Gather pay stubs and tax returns so your income is easy to document.
- **Capital:** Build a cash reserve and keep statements organized. Write down your assets and debts in one place so your net worth is clear.
- **Collateral:** If the loan is secured, understand the asset's value before you apply. A larger down payment gives the lender more cushion.
- **Conditions:** Ask for a realistic amount for a defined purpose. A smaller request, or a different term, can turn a borderline application into an approvable one.

**If you're turned down:** The lender must send an adverse-action notice naming the credit reporting company it used. The CFPB says you can [get a free report from that company](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-get-a-free-copy-of-my-credit-reports-en-5/) if you ask within 60 days of the notice. Check it for errors, fix what you can, and reapply once the weakest C is stronger.

## Applying for a mortgage?

A home loan uses the same five C's, but with program-specific rules on credit scores, debt-to-income limits, down payments and appraisals. According to the [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/does-my-credit-score-affect-my-ability-to-get-a-mortgage-loan-or-the-mortgage-rate-i-pay-en-319/), a mortgage lender weighs your credit score along with your credit report, your history with that lender, your existing debt, your savings, your total assets and your current income.

For the mortgage-specific version, read [what banks look for when reviewing a mortgage application](/what-do-banks-look-for-when-reviewing-a-mortgage-application/).
