Mortgage lenders look for a pattern of on-time payments, how much of your available credit you're using, recent new accounts and inquiries, and serious negative items such as collections, foreclosures or bankruptcies on your Equifax, Experian and TransUnion credit reports.

Your credit report is the raw record. Your credit score is a number calculated from it. A lender reads both, so cleaning up the report before you apply is one of the most useful things you can do.

This guide covers what is on the report, what gets flagged, which reports and score models lenders use, and the minimum scores that apply to the main loan programs. It is not a complete overview of how lenders decide. For that, see the [broader overview of what lenders review](/what-do-banks-look-for-when-reviewing-a-mortgage-application/).

## What mortgage lenders check on your credit report

Six things matter most when a lender reads your report:

- **Payment history.** Did you pay your accounts on time? Late and missed payments are the clearest warning sign. [myFICO](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/whats-in-your-credit-score) says payment history makes up 35% of a FICO Score, the largest single category.
- **Balances compared with credit limits.** The report shows each card's limit and balance. If you're using a high percentage of your available credit, lenders may assume you're overextended. This "amounts owed" category is 30% of a FICO Score.
- **Collection items.** The CFPB lists missed payments and loans sent to collections as collection items on a report.
- **Bankruptcies and foreclosures.** The CFPB lists bankruptcies as public records, and an account's status can show a foreclosure.
- **New accounts and hard inquiries.** Opening several accounts in a short period signals risk, especially with a short credit history. New credit is 10% of a FICO Score.
- **Account age and mix.** A longer history helps (15% of a FICO Score), and a mix of credit types counts for a little (10%). myFICO says you don't need one of each type.

A quick example of balances versus limits (hypothetical): a card with a $10,000 limit and a $3,000 balance is using 30% of its limit. Paying the balance down to $1,000 brings that to 10%. Same card, same income, but a very different picture of how stretched you are.

## Which credit reports and scores do mortgage lenders use?

### All three bureaus and the middle score

You have more than one credit report, because each credit reporting company collects its own data and creditors aren't required to report to every one. According to the [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/does-my-credit-score-affect-my-ability-to-get-a-mortgage-loan-or-the-mortgage-rate-i-pay-en-319/), most mortgage lenders look at scores from all three of the major credit reporting companies (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion) and use the middle score to decide what rate to offer.

For example, if your three scores are 702, 715 and 728, the middle score is 715. A lender using the middle score wouldn't use the 728 or the 702.

This is also why the three reports are sometimes called a "tri-merge" report: the lender orders one report that merges the data from all three bureaus. Fannie Mae's [Selling Guide](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-5.1-01/general-requirements-credit-scores) describes the same thing. For loans run through its Desktop Underwriter (DU) system, the lender must request credit scores for each borrower from each of the three repositories on the merged report.

### Which score model (classic FICO or VantageScore 4.0)

The score in your banking app or a free monitoring site is often not the score a mortgage lender uses. The CFPB says you have many different credit scores and many ways to get one.

Fannie Mae's guide (version dated 11/04/2026) lists the scores it accepts:

- Classic FICO versions: Equifax Beacon 5.0, Experian/Fair Isaac Risk Model V2 and TransUnion FICO Risk Score, Classic 04.
- VantageScore 4.0 from Equifax, Experian and TransUnion.

The lender requests three scores under either classic FICO or VantageScore 4.0, and the same model must be used for all borrowers on a single loan. So your app's number may differ from the one on your loan file, even if both are accurate. The guide also notes that FICO Score 10T is approved for future use but isn't yet deliverable, so check with your lender about the model in use.

## What information appears on a credit report

The CFPB describes five sections on a typical [credit report](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-report-en-309/):

1. **Personal information:** names you've used, current and former addresses, birth date, Social Security number and phone numbers.
2. **Credit accounts:** current and past accounts with the type (mortgage, installment, revolving), status, limit or amount, balance, payment history, open and close dates and the creditor's name.
3. **Collection items:** missed payments, loans sent to collections and certain overdue child support information.
4. **Public records:** bankruptcies.
5. **Inquiries:** companies that have accessed your report.

Income is not on the list. Your report doesn't show your salary or employer, and myFICO says salary, occupation and employment history aren't factors in a FICO Score. Lenders verify income separately, using documents such as pay stubs and tax returns. That's one reason a strong score doesn't guarantee approval: the lender also checks how much of your income your debts take up (your [debt-to-income ratio](/how-to-calculate-debt-to-income-ratio/)).

## The red flags: what each item signals

Each negative item tells the lender something slightly different:

- **Late payments:** you may struggle to pay on time. Recent and repeated lates matter more than a single old one. (myFICO notes a few late payments aren't an automatic score-killer when the rest of the history is good.)
- **Collections:** an account went unpaid long enough to be handed to a collector.
- **Charge-offs:** a creditor wrote an account off as a loss after prolonged non-payment.
- **Foreclosure:** a previous mortgage ended with the lender taking the property.
- **Bankruptcy:** you went through a court process to discharge or restructure debts.
- **Maxed-out cards:** balances near limits suggest little room for a new payment.

Serious events like foreclosure and bankruptcy usually come with a waiting period before you can qualify again, and the length depends on the program, the type of event and the circumstances. Don't rely on a number from a blog post. Fannie Mae publishes its rules in [Selling Guide B3-5.3-07](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-5.3-07/significant-derogatory-credit-events-waiting-periods-and-re-establishing-credit), and FHA, VA and USDA publish their own. Ask your lender which applies to you.

## Minimum credit scores by loan type

Program minimums are floors, not guarantees. Individual lenders may set higher requirements, and your score also affects your rate.

| Program | Minimum credit score rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Fannie Mae, manually underwritten | 620 for fixed-rate loans, 640 for adjustable-rate mortgages (ARMs) | [Selling Guide B3-5.1-01](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-5.1-01/general-requirements-credit-scores) |
| Fannie Mae, Desktop Underwriter (DU) | No minimum score required; DU assesses the full credit report | [Selling Guide B3-5.1-01](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-5.1-01/general-requirements-credit-scores) |
| FHA | 580 or above: eligible for maximum financing. 500 to 579: maximum 90% loan-to-value | [HUD Mortgagee Letter 2026-11](https://www.hud.gov/sites/default/files/hudclips/documents/2026-11hsgml.pdf) |

Two details matter here. First, Fannie Mae's manual-underwriting requirements are published in its Eligibility Matrix and also vary with loan-to-value, loan purpose, number of units, amortization type and debt-to-income ratio, so your lender can tell you what applies to your exact loan. Second, FHA's tiers come from HUD's Mortgagee Letter 2026-11 on the minimum decision credit score. At 580 or above, a borrower is eligible for maximum financing (generally a 3.5% down payment on an FHA purchase). Between 500 and 579, financing is limited to a maximum loan-to-value ratio of 90%, which means a down payment of at least 10%.

VA and USDA loans have their own credit guidelines, and many lenders add stricter rules of their own. Ask a lender who offers the program you're considering.

## How to check your credit reports before you apply

1. **Get your reports.** The CFPB says you can view your free reports from Equifax, Experian and TransUnion once a week at [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com), and you also have the right to one free report from each company every 12 months. It's the official source.
2. **Read all three.** Data can differ between bureaus because creditors don't have to report to all of them.
3. **Look for errors.** Check names, addresses, accounts you don't recognize, wrong balances and payments marked late that weren't. The CFPB warns that errors can lower your score and mean a higher rate, and recommends correcting them well before you apply.
4. **Dispute what's wrong.** Start with the CFPB's guide on [how to dispute an error](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-dispute-an-error-on-my-credit-report-en-314/). Allow time: you want any fixes to be reflected before a lender pulls your credit.

Checking your own report doesn't hurt your score. The CFPB says the credit reporting companies don't treat it like a lender's inquiry. See the timeline in [when to start looking for a mortgage](/when-to-start-looking-for-a-mortgage/).

## What not to do before and during the mortgage process

- **Don't apply for new credit.** The CFPB advises avoiding new credit cards, car loans and other loans right before or during the mortgage process, because each application adds an inquiry that can lower your scores.
- **Don't open many accounts quickly.** The CFPB says scores can fall when you apply for or open many new accounts in a short time.
- **Do shop around within the window.** Multiple credit checks from mortgage lenders within a 45-day window are recorded as a single inquiry, so comparing offers won't stack inquiries. The CFPB says you can get multiple preapprovals and official Loan Estimates, and the effect on your credit is the same regardless of how many lenders you talk to, as long as the last check falls within 45 days of the first.
- **Don't be alarmed by the inquiry itself.** An inquiry typically has a small negative effect. myFICO says FICO Scores consider only inquiries from the last 12 months, though they stay on the report for two years.

## Credit is only one piece

Even a clean report isn't a full approval. A lender also verifies income, calculates how much of it your debts use, checks your savings and orders an appraisal.
