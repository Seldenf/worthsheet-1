A personal financial statement is not part of the standard home-purchase mortgage application, which already collects your assets and liabilities on the Uniform Residential Loan Application, but some lenders ask for one and SBA loans require one from every 20%+ owner and guarantor.

If a bank or advisor has asked you for a PFS, this guide explains why, what the lender already collects, and how to prepare one quickly.

## The short answer

- **Standard home-purchase mortgage:** no PFS is built into the application. The Uniform Residential Loan Application (URLA) has its own assets and liabilities section.
- **Some lenders, some situations:** a lender may ask for a PFS as a supplement. That is lender-specific, so ask your loan officer.
- **SBA 7(a), 504 and surety loans:** a PFS is required from each owner of 20% or more and each guarantor.

## What a standard mortgage application collects

The URLA is Freddie Mac Form 65 and Fannie Mae Form 1003. According to Fannie Mae's [instructions](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/17391/display), it has these sections:

1. **Borrower Information:** personal details, current and previous employment and income.
2. **Financial Information, Assets and Liabilities:** each bank, retirement and other account with its current value, other assets and credits, and all personal debts, leases and other liabilities.
3. **Financial Information, Real Estate:** property you own, its estimated value and the debts and expenses tied to it.
4. **Loan and Property Information:** the loan amount and purpose and the property you are buying or refinancing.
5. **Declarations:** questions about the property, how it will be financed and your financial history, including whether you are a co-signer or guarantor on any debt not disclosed on the application.

The form also has acknowledgments, military service, demographic and loan originator sections. The instructions say Section 2 should include debts not listed on your credit report, debts with deferred payments and debts you will pay off at or before closing.

You do not need all of this to start. The CFPB says that to get a Loan Estimate you submit only six items: your name, income, Social Security number, the property address, an estimated value and the loan amount. Once you proceed, the lender may ask for additional information and documents to verify what you submitted.

That verification is the point. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide requires the lender to confirm that the information you provide is accurate and complete and to document the credit history, employment, income, assets and other financial information used to qualify you. A lender that already collects and verifies assets and liabilities does not need a PFS to see them.

## When a lender may ask for a personal financial statement

We found no government or agency source that says which residential lenders request a PFS. Treat the situations below as questions to ask, not rules or statistics:

- **Private or portfolio banks:** lenders that keep loans on their own books may set their own documentation.
- **Jumbo loans:** large loans may come with additional financial documentation.
- **Self-employed or business-owner borrowers:** a lender may want to see personal finances next to business records.
- **Investment-property portfolios:** if you own several properties, a summary can be easier to read than a long list.
- **Co-signers and guarantors:** the lender may want a fuller picture of anyone backing the loan.
- **Financial advisors:** an advisor who builds a balance sheet for you will ask for the same data.

The honest approach is to ask: "Beyond the standard application, which documents do you need from me, and do you want a personal financial statement or will your own form do?" Get the answer in writing if you can.

## PFS vs. mortgage application

| | Mortgage application (URLA) | Personal financial statement |
|---|---|---|
| Who prepares it | You complete it, usually with the lender, who then verifies it | You (or an accountant), sometimes on a lender-supplied form |
| As-of date | Application date; the lender updates and verifies documents during underwriting | One stated date you choose |
| Valuations | Current account values and estimated property values | Assets at current market value, often itemized by institution |
| Contingent liabilities | Declarations ask about undisclosed co-signed or guaranteed debt | Commonly a dedicated section (SBA Form 413 has one) |
| Purpose | Request one specific mortgage | Summarize your overall financial position for any lender, advisor or program |

For a definition of a PFS, see [what is a personal financial statement](/what-is-a-personal-financial-statement/).

## SBA and business loans: Form 413 is required

Business loans work differently. SBA Form 413 is required for each proprietor, general partner, LLC managing member, owner of 20% or more of the equity, and guarantor in 7(a), 504 and surety applications. You return it to the lender (7(a)), the Certified Development Company (504) or the surety, not to SBA.

For these programs, SBA accepts other formats containing the same data, such as a lender's equivalent form or an accountant-prepared statement, according to its information notice. 8(a) and WOSB applicants must use Form 413. Timing and signature rules are changing, so confirm them with your lender. See our guides to [SBA Form 413](/sba-form-413/) and [how to fill it out](/how-to-fill-out-sba-form-413/).

## Why preparing one still helps

Even when no one requires it, a PFS is a useful rehearsal.

- **It catches errors before underwriting.** Matching your account balances to statements shows what a lender will find.
- **It shows net worth and liquid assets.** Net worth is total assets minus total liabilities, and liquid assets are what you can reach quickly.
- **It keeps your numbers consistent when you shop.** Each lender you apply to asks for the same facts, and one dated statement prevents contradictory answers.

For example, take hypothetical numbers:

| Assets | Amount | Liabilities | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Checking | $18,000 | Mortgage on current home | $280,000 |
| Savings | $42,000 | Auto loan | $14,500 |
| Retirement account | $96,000 | Credit cards | $3,200 |
| Home (market value) | $410,000 | | |
| **Total** | **$566,000** | **Total** | **$297,700** |

Net worth is $566,000 − $297,700 = $268,300. Liquid assets (checking plus savings) are $18,000 + $42,000 = $60,000. If you also co-signed a $25,000 loan for a relative, it does not belong in the $297,700, but it is a contingent liability. The URLA's Declarations ask about it and a PFS has a place to note it. A PFS prompts you to remember it before the lender asks.

## How to prepare one quickly

1. **Pick an as-of date and gather statements.** Use recent bank, retirement and investment statements, plus mortgage, auto, student and card statements.
2. **List assets at current market value and liabilities at current balances.** Use balances, not monthly payments. Real estate goes in at estimated market value, not what you paid.
3. **Subtract, check and sign.** Total assets minus total liabilities gives net worth. Confirm the totals match your account detail, then sign and date.
